# Section blueprints

Paragraph-level templates for a research article aimed at a high-impact journal. Use them as
scaffolds for the argument, never as sentences to paste.

## Title

- Declarative: `X drives Y through Z` / `Loss of X desynchronises Y`.
- Topical: `Y dynamics in Z` — weaker, use only when the finding cannot be stated in one clause.
- Constraints: no abbreviations the audience would not read, no colon unless the second half adds
  information, no "novel/first/towards/insight into/study of".
- Test: could a reader state the paper's claim after reading only the title? If not, rewrite.

## Abstract — six-sentence budget

1. **Context** (1 sentence): the established fact the field accepts. No history, no review.
2. **Gap** (1 sentence): what is unresolved or contradictory, framed as a question the paper answers.
3. **Approach + principal result** (1–2 sentences): what was done and the headline measurement,
   with numbers and units.
4. **Supporting evidence** (1–2 sentences): the decisive control, the mechanism, or the independent
   line of evidence that makes the result credible.
5. **Interpretation** (1 sentence): what the result means.
6. **Consequence** (1 sentence): what changes for the field, practice, or theory.

Common failures: three sentences of background; no number anywhere; a result stated with a hedge
that contradicts the main text; an abbreviation introduced only here.

## Introduction — four paragraphs

- **P1 Consensus.** The field's working model, in terms a neighbouring-field reader accepts. Every
  claim cited or self-evident.
- **P2 Tension.** The specific unresolved conflict. Name the papers on both sides. This is where the
  gap becomes a question.
- **P3 (optional) Why it cannot be settled yet.** Methodological obstacle, missing measurement,
  contested interpretation.
- **P4 Here we…** Three to four sentences: the approach, the principal finding, and why it resolves
  the tension. Must match the Results claim for claim; if Results cannot deliver it, cut it here.

Failure mode: five paragraphs of literature review. Literature belongs in P1–P2 as support for the
gap, never as a tour of the field.

## Results — one subsection per claim

Each subsection follows the same five-beat shape:

1. **Question** — one sentence, phrased as what was tested.
2. **Approach** — one sentence naming the method and n, no detail (that is Methods).
3. **Result** — the measurement with units, the statistic with its test and df, and the effect size
   or CI. Numerals and *P* values exactly as the journal requires.
4. **Control or robustness** — the observation that rules out the trivial explanation: the negative
   control, the alternative model, the independent replicate, the sensitivity analysis.
5. **Transition** — one sentence that hands off to the next subsection.

Subheadings are claims (`Loss of X reduces Y`), not labels (`Analysis of X`).
Order subsections causally: whatever establishes the phenomenon before whatever explains it.

## Discussion — four movements

1. **Answer** (1 paragraph): the answer to the Introduction's question, in two sentences, no new data
   and no new citations.
2. **Mechanism** (1–2 paragraphs): how the result arises, and how it fits or contradicts each of the
   models named in the Introduction. Contradiction must be addressed, not omitted.
3. **Limitations** (1 short paragraph, explicitly signposted): what the design cannot establish, which
   alternative remains open, what would falsify the claim. Reviewers notice its absence.
4. **Outlook** (1 paragraph): a concrete next experiment or testable prediction, not "future work is
   needed".

## Methods

- Past tense, no hedging, reproducible parameters in the text rather than hidden in a citation.
- Exact names and versions: instrument model, software version, package version, reference genome
  build, antibody catalogue number and RRID, cell-line authentication and mycoplasma status.
- Statistics: the test, the software, the unit of analysis, how n was determined, how outliers were
  handled (state the rule *before* seeing the data, or declare it as post hoc), multiple-comparison
  correction, and the definition of error bars.
- Randomisation, blinding, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and the stopping rule where applicable.
- For clinical work: registration number, ethics approval reference, consent procedure.

## Figure legends

- Sentence 1 in bold: what the reader should take from the figure — a claim, not a label.
- Then: what each panel shows, in panel order, with the meaning of every symbol, colour and
  abbreviation used in that figure.
- Then: the statistics for that figure (n, test, significance convention, error bars).
- The legend must be readable without the main text; "see text for details" is a defect.
- Scale bars belong in the image; the legend states their value and the method of calibration.

## Cover letter

See the main skill: four paragraphs — claim, fit, advance over the closest work, statements.

## Response to reviewers

One block per point: quoted point → response → exact change with location. Never merge points, never
answer a factual objection with rhetoric, and always state what the manuscript now says.
